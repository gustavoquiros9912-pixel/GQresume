PIP Global Safety

Global Product Migration & Rebranding

When PIP Global Safety acquired Honeywell’s personal protective equipment business in 2025, four established brands, North, Howard Leight, Miller and Uvex, had to move from Honeywell to PIP. That meant more than 2,000 assets in one year, including manuals, labels, insert cards, packaging, spec sheets and digital interfaces, and every one had to stay compliant in every market where it was sold.

As Lead UI/UX & Product Designer on a 10-person global design team, I started with the Americas portfolio and later expanded to Europe and Asia. My job was less about designing assets one at a time and more about setting priorities and building the reusable system that let the team move quickly while staying compliant.

01 — the challenge

A rebrand where mistakes are a safety problem

  • Volume against a fixed date. More than 2,000 assets across four brand catalogs had to switch to PIP within a year, while products kept shipping.
  • Regulated content. This is protective equipment. Certification marks, warnings and usage instructions differ by region and can’t be lost or altered when the brand changes.
  • Many hands. Ten designers on three continents, plus regional product and compliance teams, all touched the files. Without a shared system, the new brand could easily have splintered into local versions.

On a normal rebrand, a wrong label is a brand mistake. Here it could become a compliance failure.

02 — my role

Leading across continents

I owned design for the Americas portfolio from the start, then joined the Europe and Asia teams to extend the same system there.

  • Set migration priorities and grouped the work into waves
  • Designed reusable templates for manuals, labels, insert cards, packaging and spec sheets
  • Migrated the digital catalog, the spec-sheet system and the distributor portal to the PIP brand
  • Led other designers on the team and reviewed their work for brand and regional accuracy
  • Worked with regional product and compliance teams on market-specific content

03 — approach

Three decisions that made it manageable

With 2,000 assets, redesigning them one by one wasn’t possible. The real work was deciding what order to go in, what to standardize and what to leave flexible.

01

Triage before design

Operational clarity

Decision. Instead of working through the catalogs in order, I ranked assets by impact and grouped them into about 15 waves, so the products customers and distributors saw most moved first.

Trade-off. Lower-impact lines waited for later waves, but nothing was dropped. By the deadline, 100% of the portfolio had moved.

02

Templates, not one-offs

Design systems discipline

Decision. Every asset type got a reusable template, with locked brand zones (logo, color, type, grid) and flexible zones for product data and regional content.

Result. Most assets became a fill-in task instead of a design task, which is how I delivered about 15 finished assets a week myself.

03

Localize the content, not the brand

Global adaptability

Decision. The brand layer stayed fixed worldwide. Certifications, languages and warnings were adapted with each region’s product and compliance teams.

Result. Europe and Asia were rolled out on the same template system as the Americas, with regional content adapted on top of it.

05 — reflection

What I’d take forward

  • Design systems aren’t only for screens. Locked zones, flexible content and clear ownership worked on packaging and manuals just as well as on a component library.
  • At scale, decisions are the bottleneck. Settling the order and the rules early mattered more than any single design choice.
  • Reuse is what makes speed possible. Sustaining about 15 assets a week only worked because nothing was designed from scratch twice.
  • What I’d do differently: regional compliance joined after the first rounds of feedback. Next time I’d bring them in from wave one, so market-specific issues come up during design instead of in review.
